% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
We present an exact SAT-based approach to the Cyclic Bandwidth Minimization Problem (CBMP) using direct and order label range encodings, pairwise and product at-most-one (AMO) encodings, incremental bound reduction, symmetry breaking, and a Reverse Cuthill--McKee initial upper bound. The local experiment snapshot contains 179 runs on Harwell-Boeing graphs, including 28 graph instances in a comparison of CaDiCaL SAT with CP-SAT, CPLEX MIP, CPLEX CP Optimizer, and Gurobi under a 600-second limit. CaDiCaL proves optimality on 12 of those 28 instances; the other method outcomes include timeouts and recorded license or model errors. Separate encoding and ablation studies cover four instances each, and all 24 ablation runs prove optimality. The results are single-run observations and do not support statistical or general performance claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
    The Bandwidth Minimization Problem (BMP) is a well-known combinatorial optimization problem in graph theory that seeks to assign labels to the vertices of a graph such that the maximum difference between the labels of adjacent vertices is minimized \cite{wang:hal-01166658}. This problem has attracted considerable attention due to its theoretical significance and its applications in areas such as data storage, network survivability, industrial electromagnetic, saving large hypertext media \cite{wang:hal-01166658}, finite element methods, large-scale power transmission systems, circuit design, chemical kinetics, numerical geophysics \cite{PINANA2004200}.

    A natural extension of BMP is the Cyclic Bandwidth Minimization Problem (CBMP), in which each label is assigned to vertices of a cyclic graph. While CBMP shares many similarities with BMP, the cyclic nature of the labeling introduces additional complexities that distinguish it from the classical problem and require specialized solution techniques. Over the years, researchers have proposed various approaches to solve BMP and CBMP. Early studies focused on finding exact bandwidths or lower bounds for general graphs. Eventually, exact algorithms were developed that include the implementation of branch-and-bound \cite{10.1007/978-3-642-37798-3_13}, the obtaining of lower bounds using distance- and degree-related parameters \cite{ZHOU2000357}, or the derivation of sharp upper-bounds by adding new edges \cite{CHAN2008131}. Using these theoretical studies, practical solutions like tabu search \cite{RODRIGUEZTELLO201517} and iterated three-phases search (ITPS) \cite{REN765302} have been proposed and proven to be state-of-the-art for solving CBMP.
    % Exact algorithms, including branch-and-bound methods and integer programming formulations, can produce optimal solutions but often suffer from high computational costs due to the combinatorial explosion of the search space. Consequently, numerous heuristic and metaheuristic approaches, such as genetic algorithms, tabu search, variable neighborhood search, and ant colony optimization, have been developed to obtain high-quality solutions within reasonable computational times. Although these methods have demonstrated promising performance, they may not always guarantee optimality.

    This report studies an exact SAT approach to CBMP. It describes direct and order encodings for the label range constraint, pairwise and product encodings for at-most-one (AMO) constraints, incremental reduction of the bandwidth bound, rotation and reflection symmetry breaking, and a heuristic initial upper bound. The experiments compare the SAT approach with CP-SAT, CPLEX MIP, CPLEX CP Optimizer, and Gurobi, and evaluate encoding choices and ablations. The current results are from the recorded benchmark runs; this report does not claim comparisons with tabu search or ITPS.

    The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces a proper definition of CBMP and SAT. Section 3 presents the method to our exact SAT approach. Section 4 describes experiments pertaining tools, datasets and their results. Section 5 concludes and summarizes the outcome extracted.

    % Among exact solution approaches, Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) solving has emerged as a powerful alternative for tackling difficult combinatorial optimization problems. Advances in modern SAT solvers, including conflict-driven clause learning, efficient branching heuristics, and sophisticated preprocessing techniques, have significantly improved their ability to handle large and complex problem instances. By encoding CBMP as a satisfiability problem, the extensive capabilities of state-of-the-art SAT solvers can be leveraged to explore the solution space efficiently and potentially overcome some of the scalability limitations associated with traditional exact methods. This makes SAT-based approaches an attractive direction for solving CBMP.

    % The importance of CBMP extends beyond theoretical graph optimization. In practice, cyclic arrangements naturally arise in applications such as cyclic scheduling, communication network design, round-robin tournament organization, production planning, and the placement of components in ring-based architectures. For example, minimizing cyclic distances between strongly connected entities can improve communication efficiency, reduce transmission delays, and optimize resource utilization. As systems become increasingly complex, effective methods for solving CBMP can contribute to more efficient designs and improved operational performance across a wide range of domains.

\section{Background}

    \subsection{Problem Definition}
        Let $G(V,E)$ be a finite undirected graph with vertex set $V$ of size $N$, $C_N$ be a cyclic graph with $N$ vertices and an embedding (also called labeling or arrangement) $\varphi$ of $G$ in $C_N$ be one-on-one mapping from $V$ to $V$. The cyclic bandwidth is then given as:
        \begin{center}
            $C_b(G, \varphi) = \max\limits_{(u,v)\in E}{|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|_N}$
        \end{center}
        with $\varphi(u)$ being a label assigned to vertex $u$ and $|x|_N$ equaling the minimum cycle distance, or $\min(|x|, N-|x|)$.

        The Cyclic Bandwidth Minimization Problem (CBMP)'s objective is to find an embedding $\varphi^* \in Q$ that minimizes the cyclic bandwidth of the graph, where $Q$ is the set of all possible labelings. In other words, we seek an arrangement of vertices on the cyclic graph such that the \textbf{largest cyclic distance} between any two connected vertices is minimized.

        \subsection{Boolean Satisfiability}
            Boolean satisfiability problem, also commonly known as SAT, is a fundamental problem in logic and computer science. It asks whether there exists an assignment of True or False to a Boolean formula's variables to make said formula evaluate to True, in which case the formula is considered to be \textit{satisfiable}. These formulas are built from variables and operators (containing AND, OR, NOT and more) and often written in conjuctive normal form (CNF) - conjuction of several clauses, which are disjunction of literals.
                        
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{INPUT:} A propositional logic formula, represented in the form of conjunctive normal form.
                \item \textbf{OUTPUT:}
                \begin{itemize}
                    \item \textit{SAT} if there exists an assignment of truth values for each literals of the formula so that the original CNF become true.
                    \item \textit{UNSAT} if for all possible assignments of truth values for each literals of the formula, it always become false.
                \end{itemize}
            \end{itemize}

            For example:
            \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle F_1 = \neg x_1\wedge x_2\)
                \\*
                \(\displaystyle F_2 = \neg x_1 \wedge x_1\)
            \end{center}
            The formula $F_1$ becomes true with $x_1$ = false, $x_2$ = true, therefore $F_1$ is SAT. On the other hand, $F_2$ is always false, irrespective of any possible $(x_1, x_2)$ combination, thus making it UNSAT.

\section{Method}

    \subsection{Label assignment constraint}
        Each vertex $u$ is associated with $N$ label variables:

        \begin{center}
            \(\displaystyle
            X_u=\left\{x_u^1,x_u^2,\dots,x_u^N\right\}
            \)
        \end{center}
        
        where $x_u^k$ is true if and only if vertex $u$ is assigned label $k$. Given $N$ vertices and $N$ distinct labels, a valid assignment requires that each label $k$ be assigned to exactly one vertex. This Exactly One (EXO) constraint can be expressed as the conjunction of an At Most One (AMO) constraint and an At Least One (ALO) constraint over the set of variables corresponding to label k $k$. Specifically, let:

        \begin{center}
            \(\displaystyle
            X^k = \{x_1^k, x_2^k, \dots, x_N^k\}
            \)
        \end{center}
        
         denote the variables representing the assignment of label $k$ to the vertices. For each $k$, the EXO constraint is encoded as:
         
         \begin{center}
             \(\displaystyle
             \text{AMO}(X^k) \wedge \text{ALO}(X^k)
             \)
         \end{center}

        \subsubsection{At Least One (ALO)}
            A single clause can be used to express $\text{ALO}(X^k)$:

            \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle
                \text{ALO}(X^k) = \bigvee_{u=1}^{N} x_u^k.
                \)
            \end{center}

        \subsubsection{At Most One (AMO)}
            This section discusses two different approaches to encode $\text{AMO}(X^k)$:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{Pairwise:} We explicitly encode the constraint that if one variable is true, then every other variable must be false:

                \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle
                    \text{AMO}(X^k) = \bigwedge_{u=1}^{N-1}
                    \bigwedge_{v=u+1}^{N}
                    \left(x_u^k \rightarrow \neg x_v^k\right)
                    \)
                \end{center}

                Or, in CNF:
                \begin{center}
                    \(\displaystyle
                    \text{AMO}(X^k) = \bigwedge_{u=1}^{N-1}
                    \bigwedge_{v=u+1}^{N}
                    \left(\neg x_u^k \vee \neg x_v^k\right)
                    \)
                \end{center}
                
                This encoding uses no auxiliary variables but requires $\binom{N}{2} = O(N^2)$ binary clauses per label.

                \item \textbf{Product:} We arrange the $N$ variables in a $p \times q$ grid ($pq \ge N$, typically $p \approx q \approx \lceil\sqrt{N}\rceil$) and introduce row variables $r_1,\dots,r_p$ and column variables $c_1,\dots,c_q$. A variable located in cell $(a,b)$ implies both its row and its column variables:

                \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle
                x_{a,b} \rightarrow r_a \qquad \text{and} \qquad x_{a,b} \rightarrow c_b
                \)
                \end{center}    
                    We then enforce $\text{AMO}(r_1,\dots,r_p)$ and $\text{AMO}(c_1,\dots,c_q)$ recursively using the product encoding (or pairwise encoding for small instances). If two variables are assigned $True$, they must differ in either their row or column index, thereby violating one of the two AMO constraints. This encoding uses $O(\sqrt{N})$ auxiliary variables and $O(N)$ clauses per label, which is significantly fewer than the pairwise encoding for large $N$.
            \end{itemize}


    \subsection{Label range constraint}
        Given a graph $G(V,E)$ with $|V|=N$ and a bandwidth upper bound $\text{UB}$, a labeling $\varphi$ is valid if every edge satisfies a bandwidth constraint; that is, the cyclic distance between the labels of its vertices must be less than $\text{UB}$.
        
        Let $\varphi(u)=i$ and $\varphi(v)=j$. In CBMP, labels lie on a circle and the distance between two labels is:
        \begin{center}
            \(\displaystyle
        \label{eq:cyclic_distance}
            d(i,j) = |i-j|_N = \min\bigl(|i-j|,\; N-|i-j|\bigr)
        \)
        \end{center}
        
        An edge $(u,v)\in E$ satisfies the bandwidth constraint if $d(i,j) \le \text{UB}-1$ (equivalently, $d(i,j) < \text{UB}$). The general approach to encoding this constraint is to fix the label of one vertex and applying a \textbf{label range constraint} on the other vertex. Specifically, for each $i$, if $\varphi(u)=i$, then $\varphi(v) \in [i-\text{UB}+1,\, i+\text{UB}-1] \bmod N$.

        Note that since $d(i,j) = d(j,i)$, it is sufficient to encode the constraint in one direction ($u \to v$) for each edge. 

        \subsubsection{Direct Encoding}
            The direct encoding explicitly forbids every pair of labels that are too far apart. Specifically, for each edge $(u,v)\in E$ and all assignments $\varphi(u)=i$ and $\varphi(v)=j$:
            
            \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle
                \neg x_u^i \vee \neg x_v^j \qquad \forall \; 1 \le i,j \le N,\; \text{if} \; d(i,j) \geq \text{UB}.
                \)
            \end{center}

            This encoding additionally requires $\text{AMO}(X_u)$ \text{and} $\text{ALO}(X_u)$ constraints for every vertex $u$ to ensure that exactly one label is assigned to each vertex.

        \subsubsection{Order Encoding}
            The order encoding introduces $N+1$ auxiliary variables \textit{for each vertex}: 

            \begin{center}
                \(\displaystyle
                G_u=\{g_u^1, g_u^2,\dots, g_u^N, g_u^{N+1}\}
                \)
            \end{center}
            
            where $g_u^k$ is true if $\varphi(u) \ge k$. The variable $g_v^k$ expresses a left bound $\varphi(v) \geq k$, while its negation $\neg g_v^k$ expresses a right bound $\varphi(v) < k$. For example, with $N=6$ and $\varphi(u)=3$:
            \begin{center}
                \begin{tabular}{c|ccccccc}
                    label $k$ & 1                   & 2                   & 3                   & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7  \\ \hline
                    $x_u^k$   & F                   & F                   & \textit{\textbf{T}} & F & F & F & \_ \\
                    $g_u^k$   & \textit{\textbf{T}} & \textit{\textbf{T}} & \textit{\textbf{T}} & F & F & F & F  \\
                \end{tabular}
            \end{center}

            This encoding consists of two components: the consistency clauses and the biconditional clauses.

            \begin{itemize}
                \item Consistency clauses:
                \begin{center}
                    \(\displaystyle
                    g_u^{k+1} \rightarrow g_u^{k} \qquad \forall \; u\in V,\; 1 \le k < N
                    \)
                \end{center}    
                In combination with the constants $g_u^1 = \text{true}$ and $g_u^{N+1} = \text{false}$, these clauses enforce that if $\varphi(u)=k$, then $G_u$ is a series of true variables starting from the constant $g_u^1$ up until $g_u^k$, followed by a series of false variables, bounded by $g_u^{N+1}$.

                \item Biconditional clauses:

                \begin{center}
                                    \(
                \displaystyle
                                x_u^k \leftrightarrow (g_u^k \wedge \neg g_u^{k+1}) \qquad \forall \; u\in V,\; 1 \le k \le N
                \)
                \end{center}

                These clauses enforce the relationship between the auxiliary order variables and the label variables.
            \end{itemize}

            Note that, by the \textit{biconditional clauses}, $x_u^k$ is true exactly when $g_u^k$ is true \textit{and} $g_u^{k+1}$ is false, which only happens at the point where $G_u$ changes from true to false. Since the constants $g_u^1 = \text{true}$ and $g_u^{N+1} = \text{false}$ guarantee the existence of a unique transition point between true and false values between $k=1$ and $k=N$, and the \textit{consistency clauses} guarantee that such transition can only happen once, it follows that exactly one $x_u^k$ is true for each vertex $u$. Hence, the order encoding inherently guarantees that every vertex is assigned exactly one label.
            
            With this encoding, for each edge $(u,v)\in E$ and each label $i$ of $u$, and depending on whether the range of valid labels for $v$ wraps around the circle, the label range constraint can be written as follows:

            \begin{itemize}
                \item No wrap $(\text{UB} \le i \le N-\text{UB}+1)$:
    
                \begin{center}
                    \(\displaystyle
                    x_u^i \rightarrow g_v^{i-\text{UB}+1}
                    \qquad \text{and} \qquad x_u^i \rightarrow \neg g_v^{i+\text{UB}}
                    \)
                \end{center}

                \item Wrap in the left $(1 \leq i < \text{UB})$:

                \begin{center}
                                    \(\displaystyle
                x_u^i \rightarrow (\neg g_v^{i+\text{UB}} \vee g_v^{N+i-\text{UB}+1})
                \)
                \end{center}

                \item Wrap in the right

                \begin{center}
                    \(\displaystyle
                    x_u^i \rightarrow (\neg g_v^{i+\text{UB}-N} \vee g_v^{i-\text{UB}+1})
                    \)
                \end{center}    
            \end{itemize}

            In the wrapping cases, the allowed sets are a union of two intervals, $[1,\,i+\text{UB}-1] \cup [N+i-\text{UB}+1,\,N]$ when wrapping left, and $[1,\,i+\text{UB}-N-1] \cup [i-\text{UB}+1,\,N]$ when wrapping right. The two wrapping cases cannot occur simultaneously when $\text{UB} \le \left\lfloor \frac{N}{2} \right\rfloor$; a larger bound does not restrict anything, since $d(i,j) \le \left\lfloor \frac{N}{2} \right\rfloor$ always holds.

        \paragraph{\textbf{Clause counts:}}
            Assuming $\text{UB} \le \left\lfloor \frac{N}{2} \right\rfloor$, the allowed label range of $v$ contains $2\,\text{UB}-1$ labels, so for each of the $N$ potential labels $i\in [1,N]$ of $u$: the direct encoding forbids $N-(2\,\text{UB}-1)$ labels of $v$, therefore $N(N-2\,\text{UB}+1)$ clauses are required \textit{per edge}. Additionally, the direct encoding needs roughly $2N$ $\text{AMO}$ clauses (product encoding) and 1 $\text{ALO}$ clause \textit{per vertex} to ensure that each vertex is assigned exactly one label, so the total number of clauses required is about $|E|\cdot N(N-2\,\text{UB}+1) + N(2N+1)$. The order encoding takes roughly 2 clauses for each of the $N-2\,\text{UB}+2$ potential labels $i\in [\text{UB},N-\text{UB}+1]$ of $u$ that don't cause the valid label range of $v$ to wrap, plus 1 clause for each of the $2(\text{UB}-1)$ wrapping cases, therefore about $2N-2\,\text{UB}+2$ clauses are required \textit{per edge}. In addition, for each vertex $u$, it needs: roughly $N$ consistency clauses, $3N$ biconditional clauses, and 2 clauses for the constants $g_u^1$ and $g_u^{N+1}$, which brings the total to around $|E|\cdot (2N-2\,\text{UB}+2) + N(4N+2)$ clauses. This gives $O(|E|\cdot N^2)$ clauses for the direct encoding and $O(|E|\cdot N + N^2)$ for the order encoding.

    \subsection{Finding the minimum bandwidth using Incremental SAT}
        To find the minimum bandwidth of a graph, we iteratively solve SAT instances with decreasing upper bounds. The process terminates when no valid assignment exists. In SAT solving terms, we try to solve a sequence of formulas, each enforcing a smaller upper bound. Whenever a SAT solution is found, the upper bound is updated to the bandwidth of the corresponding assignment. The first UNSAT instance proves that no assignment exists with a smaller bandwidth, and therefore the current upper bound is optimal..

        Due to the monotonicity of the order encoding:


        \begin{center}
        \(\displaystyle
        g_u^{k+1} \rightarrow g_u^k
        \qquad
        \text{and}
        \qquad
        \neg g_u^k \rightarrow \neg g_u^{k+1}
        \)
        \end{center}
        
        
        the clauses for a smaller $\text{UB}$ imply those for previous, larger $\text{UB}$s:

        \begin{center}
            \(\displaystyle
            g_v^{k-\text{UB}+1} \rightarrow g_v^{k-\text{UB}}
            \qquad
            \text{and}
            \qquad
            \neg g_v^{k+\text{UB}} \rightarrow \neg 
            \)
        \end{center}
        
        Therefore, each subsequent formula is obtained by appending additional clauses that enforce a smaller upper bound. Since each formula extends the previous one, the same SAT solver instance can be reused instead of being reinitialized from scratch. This technique, known as Incremental SAT, allows the solver to retain learned clauses and heuristic information across successive formulas, often reducing solving time.

        \paragraph{\textbf{Initial upper bound.}}
            The search begins with an initial upper bound obtained from a heuristic labeling generated using the Reverse Cuthill-McKee (RCM) \cite{cuthill1969rcm} algorithm.

        \begin{algorithm}[H]
            \caption{Pseudocode of Incremental SAT for the cyclic bandwidth problem}
            \begin{algorithmic}[1]
                \State \textbf{Input}: Undirected graph $G(V,E)$ with $|V|=N$, time limit: $T_{\max}$
                \State \textbf{Output}: The best assignment $A$, and its resulting bandwidth $\text{UB}$
                \Statex
                \State $A \gets \textsc{InitialAssignment}(G)$ \Comment{heuristic assignment (RCM)}
                \State $\text{UB} \gets \textsc{Bandwidth}(A)$
                \State $\text{clauses} \gets \textsc{InitialClauses}()$ \Comment{label assignment constraints, order encoding consistency constraints,...}
                \Statex
                \While{$\textsc{TimeElapsed}() < T_{\max}$}
                    \ForAll{$(u,v) \in E$}
                        \For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
                            \State $\text{clauses}$.append( $[\varphi(u)=i] \rightarrow [\min(|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|,\, N-|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|) \le \text{UB}-1]$ )
                        \EndFor
                    \EndFor
                    \Statex
                    \State $result, model \gets \textsc{Solve}(\text{clauses}, T_{\max}-\textsc{TimeElapsed}())$
                    \If{$result$ is UNSAT \textbf{or} $\textsc{TimeElapsed}() > T_{\max}$}
                        \State \textbf{break} \Comment{UNSAT: $\text{UB}$ is minimal; timeout: best bound found}
                    \EndIf
                    \Statex
                    \State $A \gets \textsc{ExtractAssignment}(model)$
                    \State $\text{UB} \gets \textsc{Bandwidth}(A)$ \Comment{may be smaller than the enforced $\text{UB}-1$}
                \EndWhile
                \Statex
                \State \textbf{return} $A$, $\text{UB}$
            \end{algorithmic}
        \end{algorithm}

    \subsection{Symmetry breaking constraints}
        \subsubsection{Indexing convention}
        The equations in this section use labels $1,\dots,N$, as in the encodings above, while the implementation indexes labels $0,\dots,N-1$. Adding $1$ to every label does not change any cyclic distance, so the two conventions are equivalent.

        \subsubsection{Rotation symmetry breaking.}
        Since rotating a labeling does not change its cyclic bandwidth, each solution belongs to an equivalence class of $N$ rotationally symmetric labelings. Without symmetry breaking, the solver may waste time exploring all of them. To eliminate this symmetry, we fix an arbitrary anchor vertex $v_1$​ to label 1:

        \begin{center}
            \(\displaystyle
            \varphi(v_1) = 1 \leftrightarrow x_{v_1}^1.
            \)
        \end{center}
        
        Any valid assignment can be rotated to place $v_1$ at label 1 without changing the cyclic bandwidth. Therefore, this constraint removes all rotationally equivalent solutions while preserving at least one optimal solution.

        \subsubsection{Reflection symmetry breaking}
        After fixing $\varphi(v_1)=1$, the only remaining symmetry is reflection about label 1, which map label 1 to itself and label $k$ to $N+2-k$ for $2 \le k \le N$. To retain only one representative from each reflected pair of solutions, we require:
        
        \begin{center}

            \(\displaystyle
            \varphi(v_2) < \varphi(v_N)
            \)
        \end{center}
        
        Note that this requires $v_2$ and $v_N$ to be different vertices, which is true when $N \ge 3$. In the order encoding, $g_{v_2}^i$ is true if $\varphi(v_2) \ge i$. Therefore, if $\varphi(v_N) = i$, then $\varphi(v_2)<i$ must hold, which is encoded as

        \begin{center}

            \(\displaystyle
            x_{v_N}^i \rightarrow \neg g_{v_2}^i
            \leftrightarrow (\neg x_{v_N}^i \vee \neg g_{v_2}^i)
            \quad \forall i \in \{1,\dots,N\}
            \)
        \end{center}
        
        All benchmark instances in this report have $N \ge 3$, so this constraint can be applied to all of them.

    \subsection{Heuristic initial upper bound}
        To reduce the number of iterations required to reach the optimum, we start the search from an initial upper bound obtained from the RCM algorithm \citep{cuthill1969rcm}. RCM constructs a breadth-first search assignment starting from a pseudo-peripheral vertex, which is known to produce orderings with small envelope and bandwidth on sparse physical graphs.

        The RCM assignment is evaluated via Eq.~(\ref{eq:cyclic_distance}) to obtain an initial bandwidth $\text{UB}_{\text{rcm}}$. If the heuristic assignment satisfies all constraints, we set $\text{UB} = \text{UB}_{\text{rcm}}$; otherwise, the search starts from the trivial bound $\text{UB} = \left\lfloor \frac{N}{2} \right\rfloor + 1$.

    \subsection{Discussion and Limitations}

        The exact-methods batch includes large matrices up to $N=1104$, but its 600-second limit leaves many runs unresolved. CaDiCaL SAT times out on 16 of the 28 instances and proves optimality on 12, compared with 10 for CP-SAT, 7 for CPLEX CP Optimizer, 1 for CPLEX MIP, and 6 for Gurobi. CPLEX MIP and Gurobi fail for a different reason. Most of their errors (24 of 27 for CPLEX MIP, all 21 for Gurobi) come from the size limit of the edition and the license we used, so those methods did not actually perform search on these instances. Because of this, these results do not reflect the underlying performance of CPLEX or Gurobi; they mainly reflect what could be run in our environment, and the status table should be read as an account of this environment and batch.

        The same instance recurs across experiment groups under the default configuration, which gives a rough view of run-to-run variation. \texttt{will57} is solved in 5.39, 4.13, 6.27, and 3.85 seconds in the exact-methods, encoding, ablation (strong), and incumbent-progress groups, respectively, and \texttt{bcsstk01} in 17.62, 9.96, and 9.42 seconds in the first three groups (all under the default configuration). \texttt{ash85} takes 527.21 seconds in the exact-methods group but 335.64 seconds in the incumbent-progress group. These runs differ by a factor of approximately 2, so differences of this magnitude between single runs may simply be attributed to run-to-run variation. As an example, runtimes of the direct versus order encodings for the label range constraint differ by 4.6 to 26 times on the three instances where the direct encoding finishes, a much larger difference than this variation.

        For the encodings, the direct one is the slowest in all four of our instances. It takes 573.36 seconds on \texttt{bcspwr02} and 85.40 seconds on \texttt{bcsstk01}, and on \texttt{curtis54} it stops at the 600-second limit. Order + product needs 21.89 and 9.96 seconds on the first two. So the order encoding looks much faster, at least on these four graphs. This difference may stem from the clause count, as the direct encoding needs $O(|E| \cdot N^2)$ clauses compared to $O(|E| \cdot N + N^2)$ for the order encoding, although this was not verified directly.

        The ablation is less clear. Removing reflection symmetry breaking does not slow down \texttt{bcsstk01} and \texttt{will57} (5.41 and 2.11 seconds, compared to 9.42 and 6.27 seconds for the strong setting), while on \texttt{curtis54} the strong setting is the fastest of the six. Similarly, the run without the heuristic is the fastest on \texttt{bcspwr02} but the slowest on \texttt{curtis54}. So we cannot say that one component always helps. Since each setting was run only once, part of these differences may simply come from sensitivity of the solver's search heuristics to clause ordering, which was not measured directly.

\section{Conclusion}

    This work implements an exact SAT-based CBMP solver with direct and order label range encodings, pairwise and product AMO encodings, incremental bound reduction, rotation and reflection symmetry breaking, and a heuristic initial upper bound. The local snapshot contains 179 runs, including comparisons against CP-SAT, CPLEX MIP, CPLEX CP Optimizer, and Gurobi on 28 Harwell-Boeing instances. It also records encoding comparisons, six ablation settings, and incumbent progress.

    On the 28-instance comparison, CaDiCaL proves optimality on 12 instances under a 600-second limit. The encoding and ablation groups provide useful instance-level measurements, while license/model errors and unresolved timeouts limit broader comparisons. Larger runs and repeated trials would be needed to support stronger scalability and performance conclusions.
